# Project 3 Proposal — Extra Guidance and Examples

> **Optional reading:** The [Project 3 assignment](project-3.md) lists everything your proposal must include. This page offers examples and questions to help you develop your ideas. **It does not add any requirements.** You do not need to use these headings or answer every question here.

You will create your proposal as an **HTML page** and submit a link to it, as described in the main assignment. This Markdown page is only a reference guide, not a format for your submission.

Your proposal is a **plan**, not a promise that every feature will appear in the finished game. Aim for something interesting that you can realistically build and test with JavaScript and PixiJS or the browser DOM in the time available. Specific descriptions are more useful than ambitious but vague ones.

## High concept: pitch it in 1–3 sentences

Try one of these approaches:

- **Familiar game with a twist:** “It's *Pac-Man*, but the player can rearrange parts of the maze to redirect the ghosts.”
- **Combine ideas:** “A *Breakout*-style game where your paddle also builds temporary walls.”
- **Describe the main action:** “Guide a tiny robot through a factory, collecting batteries while avoiding moving machinery.”

A useful pitch tells us **what the player does** and **what makes the experience interesting**. These are invented examples, not suggestions you have to use.

## Genre and platform: help us picture the experience

Genres might include **arcade, action, puzzle, platformer, shooter, strategy, simulation, rhythm, avoidance, or exploration**. You can combine labels (for example, *arcade puzzle*). Focus on how the game plays, not just its visual theme.

For platform, describe where you intend it to work: **desktop web, mobile web, or both**. This affects controls, screen layout, and your design scope. A game designed for mouse and keyboard is different from one designed for touch.

## Story, theme, and player motivation

A game doesn't need a complicated plot—or any plot at all. Even an abstract game can have a clear goal. Consider:

- Who or what does the player control?
- What are they trying to accomplish, and why?
- What gets in their way?
- What happens when they succeed or fail?

For example: “You are a maintenance robot recovering lost parts before the factory shuts down.” That's enough premise for a simple arcade game. For an abstract puzzle, describe the goal instead.

## Aesthetics: what should it look and sound like?

Describe the **visual style** you are aiming for: pixel art, simple geometric shapes, hand-drawn, bright and playful, dark and atmospheric, etc. You can mention examples or inspiration. Also describe how sound effects, music, or other audio will contribute to the experience. **Remember that sound is required regardless of whether you use PixiJS or the browser DOM.** This is already a requirement in the main assignment; think about how sound will meaningfully add to your game rather than including it just to check a box.

It's fine if your finished art is simpler than your initial vision. A cohesive style often works better than lots of unrelated assets.

## Gameplay: the most important part

Try to describe the **core gameplay loop**: what the player does repeatedly, how the game responds, and what keeps the player going.

For example:

1. Move around the factory to collect batteries.
2. Avoid obstacles that move in predictable patterns.
3. Use collected batteries to activate doors and reach harder areas.
4. Finish the level before the timer runs out.

Also think about:

- **Controls:** What does the mouse, keyboard, or touch actually do?
- **Rules and feedback:** How does the player score points, make progress, lose, or win? How will they know what's happening?
- **Learning to play:** Will you show instructions on a title screen, introduce one mechanic at a time, or make the controls obvious from the interface?
- **Interesting choices:** Does the player decide between safe and risky routes, save or spend resources, prioritize targets, etc.?

You don't need an elaborate design document. A clear paragraph or two is usually more useful than a long list of possible features.

## Mockups: show us at least two screens

The assignment requires **at least two mockup screens**. Sketches photographed with your phone are fine. A mockup should make it easier to understand how your game would look and work—not demonstrate polished art skills.

For example, one image could show a **title/instructions screen**, and another could show **active gameplay** with the player, obstacles, score, or other interface elements. Labeling important parts can help.


## Other details and developer background

If you have special plans such as external libraries, unusual controls, asset sources, or technical challenges, mention them in **Other**. Libraries beyond the course's expected tools may need professor approval.

In **About the developer**, briefly identify yourself (name, major/minor, year) and relevant interests or skills. You do not need to be an expert at everything; it can help to identify which aspects of development you most want to practice.

## A final scope check

Before submitting, ask yourself: **Could I build a small, playable version of this idea with the time and tools available?** Try identifying the *minimum playable version*: one core mechanic, a clear goal, basic feedback, and a way to start/restart. You can expand from there if things go well.
